Dietary Guide

Vegetarian & Vegan Dining in Tokyo

Eating plant-based in Japan takes a little more planning than in many countries — but it's far from impossible. Here's what you need to know.

Japan's cuisine is built on seafood and meat, so vegetarian and vegan dining requires some extra effort — but the situation has improved significantly in recent years, and Tokyo now has a growing number of plant-based options. The main challenge isn't the absence of vegetables; it's the hidden animal ingredients.

The dashi issue

The single most important thing to understand: dashi (出汁), the foundational stock of Japanese cooking, is most commonly made from bonito flakes (katsuobushi — dried fermented tuna) or sometimes dried sardines. It appears in almost everything — miso soup, noodle broths, simmered dishes, okonomiyaki batter, even seemingly vegetable dishes. A dish that looks vegetarian may contain dashi.

  • Miso soup — almost always contains dashi (bonito).
  • Ramen and udon broth — even "vegetable" broths often use bonito.
  • Tempura batter — usually egg-based, but the dipping sauce (tentsuyu) contains dashi.
  • Okonomiyaki and takoyaki — batter contains dashi; toppings are usually seafood or pork.
  • Soy sauce — generally vegan (check for bonito in some artisan varieties).
  • Rice — plain rice (gohan) is vegan; sushi rice is vegan (vinegar, salt, sugar) unless mixed with dashi.

How to communicate your needs

Most small restaurant staff have limited English, so a written note works best. Showing a short, clear message in Japanese is far more effective than a long explanation:

  • Vegetarian: 「肉と魚は食べられません」(niku to sakana wa taberaremasen) — "I can't eat meat or fish."
  • Vegan: 「肉、魚、卵、乳製品、だしは食べられません」(niku, sakana, tamagyu, nyu-seihin, dashi wa taberaremasen) — "I can't eat meat, fish, eggs, dairy, or dashi."
  • Always add: 「だし（カツオ）は使えますか？」(dashi (katsuō) wa tsukaemasu ka?) — "Can you make it without dashi (bonito)?"

What's naturally plant-based

Several classic Japanese foods are naturally vegan or easily made so:

  • Zaru soba — cold buckwheat noodles with a dipping sauce (ask for the sauce without dashi, or use soy sauce).
  • Vegetable tempura — ask for tempura without the tentsuyu dipping sauce, or with salt.
  • Inari sushi — sushi rice in a sweet tofu pocket (naturally vegan).
  • Vegetable gyoza — many shops offer vegetable dumplings (confirm the filling).
  • Rice bowls — a simple bowl of rice with pickles or grilled vegetables.
  • Onigiri — convenience-store rice balls; umeboshi (pickled plum) and kombu (kelp) fillings are vegan.

From our directory

One restaurant in our directory is explicitly tagged as vegetarian-friendly: Afuri in Ebisu, the yuzu-ramen shop. Beyond that, several restaurants can accommodate vegetarians with notice — particularly sushi counters (where you can order vegetable and tamago nigiri), soba shops (cold soba with soy sauce), and izakayas (where you can order individual vegetable dishes). For fully plant-based dining, Tokyo also has a growing number of dedicated vegan restaurants — search "vegan Tokyo" for current options, as this scene changes quickly.

Practical tips

  • Book ahead and tell them. Email or call ahead to confirm a vegetarian option is possible — most restaurants appreciate the notice.
  • Department store food halls (depachika) have excellent vegetable sides, salads, and fruit that are clearly labeled.
  • Convenience stores are surprisingly good: onigiri, salads, edamame, and vegetable sushi are all vegan.
  • Look for 精進料理 (shojin ryori) — traditional Buddhist temple cuisine, fully vegan, available at some restaurants and temples.
