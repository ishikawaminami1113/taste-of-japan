Seasonal Guide

What to Eat in Japan by Season

Japanese cuisine follows the seasons closely — the best time to eat something is often the only time you can. Here's a month-by-month guide to what's in season.

In Japan, seasonality (旬, shun) is a core principle of cooking. Menus change with the calendar, and ingredients are celebrated at their peak — then largely disappear until next year. If you're visiting, here's what to look for.

Spring (March–May)

  • Bamboo shoots (takenoko) — tender young shoots appear in March and April, grilled or simmered as a seasonal delicacy.
  • Sakura mochi and cherry-flavored sweets — pink, wrapped in a pickled cherry leaf; sold everywhere during cherry-blossom season.
  • Strawberries — Japan's famous sweet, large strawberries peak in spring, especially in parfaits.
  • Spring vegetables — fuki (butterbur), nanohana (rapeseed blossoms), and fresh wasabi.
  • Cherry-blossom season — hanami (flower viewing) bento boxes and seasonal treats are a highlight.

Summer (June–August)

  • Unagi (freshwater eel) — traditionally eaten on the midsummer Day of the Ox (doyo no ushi no hi) to beat the heat; served over rice as unaju.
  • Cold noodles — hiyashi chuka (cold ramen), zaru soba (chilled buckwheat noodles), and cold udon are summer staples.
  • Watermelon, peaches, and grapes — Japanese fruit is a luxury; seasonal fruit parfaits are everywhere.
  • Summer festivals — yatai food stalls appear at festivals: yakitori, kakigori (shaved ice), and grilled corn.
  • Hamo (pike conger) — a Kyoto summer specialty, often as shabu-shabu or sashimi.

Autumn (September–November)

  • Sanma (Pacific saury) — the iconic autumn fish, grilled whole with salt and served with grated daikon and soy sauce.
  • Matsutake mushrooms — fragrant pine mushrooms, prized in soup and grilled dishes, peak in October–November.
  • Sweet potatoes (satsumaimo) — roasted sweet potatoes are a classic autumn street food; also in desserts.
  • Persimmons and chestnuts — kuri (chestnuts) appear in rice dishes and sweets.
  • Seasonal sushi — autumn fish like sanma and mackerel (saba) reach their peak flavor.

Winter (December–February)

  • Oden — a hot-pot stew of fish cakes, daikon, eggs, and konnyaku simmered in dashi; sold at convenience stores and specialty shops.
  • Nabe (hot pot) — sukiyaki, shabu-shabu, and chanko nabe; communal hot-pot dining is a winter institution.
  • Fugu (pufferfish) — the famous winter delicacy, served as sashimi, in hot pot, or fried (only by licensed chefs).
  • Mikan and winter citrus — sweet mandarins appear everywhere, often eaten in front of the kotatsu.
  • New Year foods — osechi ryori (layered lacquer boxes) and ozoni (m soup with rice cakes) are eaten at New Year.

Planning your visit

The easiest way to experience seasonality is to look for what's on the menu — seasonal specials (旬の食材) are often highlighted at restaurants. Ask staff what's in season when you arrive; they'll happily point you to the freshest dishes. And if you're visiting for a specific food, timing matters: unagi in midsummer, sanma in autumn, and oden in winter are the classics.
